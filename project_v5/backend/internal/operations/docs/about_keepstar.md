# What Keepstar is

Keepstar is an interface runtime: software that assembles working software
for your business out of three planes — your **data**, a set of validated
**operations**, and a generated **interface** — and keeps all three alive in
one conversation.

Instead of buying a website builder, a CRM, and a form tool and wiring them
together, you describe your business in chat. Keepstar proposes a plan from
its library of proven parts, you approve it, and it builds a working
workspace: a public storefront where your customers browse and act, and a
staff CRM where you see and handle what they did.

## The three planes

**Data.** You upload what you already have — a spreadsheet (CSV) or a JSON
export. Keepstar maps the columns, keeps every attribute even if it has
never seen the name before, and makes the records searchable immediately.
Your catalog stays yours: items, prices, photos, custom fields.

**Operations.** An operation is a real, executable capability with strict
inputs and outputs: search the catalog, create a request, book a time slot,
advance a record through a pipeline, notify staff. Operations come from a
library, are configured for your business (your entities, your statuses,
your business hours) — never improvised. When the assistant in your
storefront does something, it runs one of these operations; it cannot
invent an action that does not exist.

**Interface.** Screens are assembled from a library of proven interface
parts — cards, lists, detail views, forms — with your real data bound in by
a deterministic engine. The language model decides *what* to show; the
engine renders it from your records. Numbers, prices, and titles on screen
come from your data, not from the model's imagination.

## What you actually get

- **A public storefront** at its own URL. Visitors ask for what they want
  in plain language ("two-bedroom under 2000", "something for a birthday")
  and get real matching items, rendered as browsable cards and detail
  pages. They can act — leave a request, book a time — through forms that
  create real records.
- **A staff CRM** at a private URL. You ask it questions the same way —
  "any new requests?" — and see the records your storefront produced, tied
  to the items they are about. You advance them through your pipeline
  ("mark it contacted") in chat.
- **Automations.** When a record arrives or changes status, staff get
  notified. No one polls a dashboard.
- **Your data model.** The records your business collects — requests,
  bookings, applications — are typed and validated: statuses come from a
  closed list you approved, dates are checked, phone numbers are phone
  numbers.

## How assembly works

1. **You describe your business** — what you sell or do, and what you need.
2. **Keepstar proposes a plan**: the workspace, your record types and
   pipeline, the operations it will enable, the design system, and the
   upload door for your data. Everything in the plan comes from the
   library — every item is something that actually runs.
3. **You approve** — by saying so, or simply by acting: registering your
   account or uploading your file applies the plan too. Nothing is built
   until you do; everything is built exactly as proposed when you do.
4. **You get the addresses**: your storefront URL and your CRM URL, live,
   with your data in them. Verify by using them.

## What Keepstar is honest about

- The plan is assembled from the library. If a capability is not in the
  library, Keepstar will say so rather than pretend.
- Uploads are a CSV with a header row or a JSON array of flat objects —
  that covers most exports, but it is a real constraint.
- Notifications today live in your CRM; email and SMS delivery come later.
- The CRM link is a private tokened URL, not a full login system yet.
- It works for any business — retail, services, rentals, agencies. The
  library grows by vertical; what you can be offered is exactly what the
  library holds today.

No plugins to install, no templates to fight, no code to maintain. You
talk; the runtime assembles; what you approve is what runs.
